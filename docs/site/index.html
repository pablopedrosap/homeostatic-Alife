Homeostatic Autonomy
Pablo Pedrosa · independent research · homeostatic robotics

Robots that look after their own bodies.

A robot that only chases its task can run itself into the ground: motors overheat, a weak leg gets overloaded, and it falls. Animals work differently. Much of what a body does is keeping itself working. This project builds robot controllers that feel their own body and act to protect it, and tests them with pass criteria written down before the data existed.

Experiment 1 · Unitree Go2 quadruped · real motor-heat physics ·

Does a robot that manages its own motor heat keep walking when it gets damaged?

Simulated Unitree Go2 (official model from DeepMind's MuJoCo Menagerie, with Unitree's real torque limits). Every motor heats up with the torque it produces. When a motor gets too hot, its driver cuts the torque it can deliver, and at the limit the leg goes limp, as on the real robot. Each robot is sent on 3-minute missions carrying nothing, 3 kg or 6 kg. Every controller uses the same trained walking policy. The only difference is how each one decides how fast to go: always full speed; a simple rule that slows down when hot; or a central governor that has learned its own body and plans its pace.

Where this is going

From "feels its own body" to "protects itself on real hardware"

Nobody can measure whether a machine has experiences, so this project doesn't claim that. What can be built and tested are the working parts people usually mean when they talk about a robot being aware of itself:

  1. It feels its own body. Motor temperatures, energy, damage as inputs. built, in simulation
  2. It acts to keep its body working. Self-regulation that pays off when something breaks. partly: a third less motor wear, at a cost in distance
  3. It knows what it can do right now ("my left knee is weak") and changes its plan. in a limited form: learns its own heat physics, notices a payload
  4. It does all of this on a real robot. One quadruped that keeps walking with a failing motor. goal

Side experiment · does evolution discover self-sensing on its own? ·

No robot here. Tiny simulated creatures evolve with no reward and no goal. Those that overheat or starve die, and those that eat enough split in two. Blue ones can feel their own temperature, energy and damage. Orange ones feel a random other creature's body instead.

Try it: who wins?

150 founders with random neural controllers. Half feel their own energy, temperature and damage. The other half are yoked: they feel a random partner's body instead, so they get the same kind of signal with no information about themselves. No reward and no fitness score exist anywhere. Organisms that overheat, starve or take too much damage die. Organisms that gather enough energy split in two, passing on their genome with small mutations.

How the work is done

Pass criteria are written before the data exists

Each experiment has a pre-registration file pushed to GitHub before its results existed: the frozen physics, the seeds, the exact statistical test and the threshold. Every seed is reported. Failed hypotheses stay in the record, and earlier analyses that turned out to be confounded are corrected in the open.

Code, data and pre-registrations: github.com/pablopedrosap/homeostatic-Alife
The live world is a JavaScript port of homeostatic_colony/alife/emergent_world.py with the same constants and update order. It matches the Python statistically, not step for step. All reported results come from the Python runs.
